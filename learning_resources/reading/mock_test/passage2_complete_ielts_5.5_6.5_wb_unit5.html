Russia's boreal forests and wild grasses could combat climate change

A. Scientists assert that Russia's ancient boreal forests are a crucial natural defense against climate change, despite the risk of carbon release from the vast subterranean stores. A recent study highlights that these permafrost regions hold roughly half of the world's soil carbon, with Russia containing about two-thirds of this amount. Josep Canadell, a co-author of the study, warns that thawing permafrost could increase carbon emissions by ten percent annually over several centuries, significantly exceeding past predictions. He emphasizes that while mitigating these emissions will be expensive, the cost of inaction will be even higher.

B. The study, published in *Global Biogeochemical Cycles*, reveals that this region contains 1,672 billion tons of organic carbon, primarily stored deep underground, representing approximately half of the global below-ground organic carbon. Furthermore, a paper in *Nature* challenges the traditional view that old-growth forests are carbon-neutral, demonstrating that they continue to accumulate carbon. Sebastiaan Luyssaert, a biologist at the University of Antwerp, notes that these forests absorb more carbon than is released through natural disturbances like fires and insect infestations. Canadell, executive director of the Global Carbon Project, adds that Russia's boreal forests absorb 500 million tons of carbon annually, roughly one-fifth of the total absorbed by the world's landmass.

C. Jing Ming Chen, a geography professor at the University of Toronto, points out the long-term consequences of logging in the boreal region. He explains that removing trees releases carbon into the atmosphere, and it takes 50 to 100 years for that carbon to be sequestered back into the ground. Luyssaert and Chen advocate for the preservation of these old-growth forests to maintain their carbon storage capacity. Luyssaert stresses the urgency of controlling emissions in the next few decades to prevent irreversible consequences like permafrost melting. Nigel Roulet, a carbon cycle specialist at McGill University, anticipates that as the region warms, increased accessibility will heighten pressure on these forests, increasing their value as other resources dwindle.

D. Scientists also propose that Russia and Kazakhstan could leverage wild grasses growing on abandoned agricultural lands to combat global warming. Nicolas Vuichard, lead author of a study in *Environmental Science and Technology*, suggests these grasses could be harvested to produce ethanol or burned in coal-fueled power plants. Converting these grasses to ethanol could sequester about 10 million tons of carbon annually over 60 years. While this represents a small fraction of global fossil fuel emissions, Vuichard considers it a substantial contribution.

E. Renton Righelato, a visiting research fellow at the University of Reading, agrees that biofuels are not a practical long-term replacement for transport fuels, given the immense land requirements. However, he acknowledges that using grasses from abandoned croplands as biofuels could be beneficial. Adam Wolf, a study co-author from the Carnegie Institution for Science, cites research by Elliott Campbell in *Science* magazine, indicating that burning grasses in a coal-fueled plant doubles the carbon emission savings compared to ethanol production. Wolf advocates for using these grasses to generate electricity, offsetting coal use. He notes that both Russia and Kazakhstan have existing coal-fueled power plants, allowing for rapid implementation, potentially making them leaders in green energy exports.
