[22:43] Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. Today, in this second lecture of the series, we turn our attention to transportation. In particular, we're going to take a look at the way the motor car influenced urban development in Australia and changed our lifestyle from the 1940s to the end of the 1990s.

[23:02] Lecturer: The car is so common nowadays that we all take it for granted and we barely think about the major effect it has had on our society. But let's go back for a while to the late 1940s. Although they had been around since the start of the 20th century, only one person in eight owned a car in Australia's major cities at that time.

[23:23] Lecturer: Nearly 60% of journeys were still made by various means of public (Q31) transport, and only 15% by car, which is clearly very few compared to statistics now.

[23:36] Lecturer: Buses were very crowded in the days before extensive car use, and they were also considered to be uncomfortable (Q32) despite providing a cheap option that most people could afford.

[23:48] Lecturer: Remember, this wasn't long after the Second World War. People were tired of living without things, of making do, and to them cars represented freedom (Q33).

[23:58] Lecturer: But even for the wealthy few who did have vehicles, it wasn't easy to run a car at this time. As well as the expense of buying a car, spare parts were difficult to get, as many were imported.

[24:12] Lecturer: Another problem for car owners was that fuel was still rationed, and car taxes (Q34) were very high.

[24:19] Lecturer: Strong public feeling on issues like these from various pressure groups resulted in a change of government (Q35) in Australia after the general election in 1949, which undoubtedly shaped the future of our nation.

[24:37] Lecturer: In the 1960s, cheaper, more reliable cars became available, and this coincided with the rapid expansion of the suburbs. The car enabled these to grow in all directions, far beyond the train lines laid down in the 19th century. For the previous 150 years, the city centre had been a prestigious area, but the addition of cars made the main streets congested.

[25:03] Lecturer: This wasn't the only problem. Now they were polluted (Q36) as well. Since these streets were usually straight, they tempted motorists to speed. So the layout of newer streets was affected by this change towards private transport. In the newer areas, roads were no longer built straight. Instead, they had curves that were specially designed to force drivers to slow down. This meant they were longer, but this was not a problem because people rarely walked anymore.

[25:34] Lecturer: By the 1970s, the type of roads being built changed, with more lanes (Q37) in each direction. There were more commuters, because now there was faster access to the city where they worked.

[25:47] Lecturer: By this time, the motorcar was affecting many different areas of life. It even, surprisingly, affected relationships (Q38). Before this, meeting anyone who lived beyond walking or cycling distance was impractical, if not impossible. The car brought people together.

[26:06] Lecturer: What other effects did the car have? The increasing car use changed the way we advertise. Previously, advertisements had been designed to attract slow-moving pedestrians, so they were located at eye level and designed on a human scale.

[26:22] By the late 1970s, massive advertisements (Q39) were designed to attract the notice of the passing motorist.

[26:31] Lecturer: In the 1980s and 90s, even the location of shops began to change. We built shopping malls (Q40) out in the suburbs, which meant that old town centres did far less business. Next, we saw the development of drive-in shopping centres, theatres and even restaurants, which now ensure we get out of our cars as little as possible. Now, I'd like to turn your attention to a few statistics related to car ownership over the same period.
